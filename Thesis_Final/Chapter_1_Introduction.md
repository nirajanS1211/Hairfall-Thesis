# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 Introduction

Alopecia, the clinical term for pathological hair loss, affects individuals across a broad range of ages and demographic backgrounds worldwide. Some degree of hair shedding is a normal part of the follicular growth cycle, but a marked or sudden increase in shedding often points to an underlying disturbance in the body's systems, commonly hormonal imbalance, significant nutrient depletion, prolonged physical strain, or sustained psychological stress (Bai et al., 2026; Guo & Katta, 2017; Hussein et al., 2023). Beyond its visible effects, noticeable hair loss frequently carries a real emotional cost, contributing to lowered self-confidence, elevated anxiety, and a reduced sense of overall well-being. In this proposal, "hair loss" and "hair fall" are used with the same meaning, and "hair fall" is also the name of the target variable in the dataset.

Determining an individual's likelihood of experiencing hair loss is inherently difficult because no single biomarker reliably predicts it. Risk instead emerges from the combined, non-linear interaction of physiological traits, everyday habits, environmental factors, and pre-existing health conditions. Elements such as diet quality, sleep regularity, scalp care practices, thyroid activity, iron levels, and hereditary predisposition all interact at once, making it impractical for clinicians or individuals to gauge overall risk through observation alone.

Healthcare systems today generate large volumes of patient data through Electronic Health Records (EHRs), capturing detailed clinical histories over time. A substantial share of this clinical data is inherently tabular in structure: laboratory results, vital signs, diagnostic codes, medication histories, and patient demographics are all naturally represented as structured rows and columns rather than as free text or medical imagery. Consequently, tabular data forms the backbone of a large fraction of real-world clinical prediction tasks, including disease risk stratification, treatment outcome prediction, and, as in this study, early screening for conditions such as hair loss. Machine learning offers a way to surface patterns within this dense, multi-source tabular data that would otherwise go unnoticed. Gradient boosting methods have long been the standard tool for prediction on structured, tabular data (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018). More recently, however, tabular foundation models, which are transformer architectures pretrained to perform prediction directly from context without task-specific training, have begun to reshape this space. Prior Labs' TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2025) and Google's TabFM (Kong et al., 2026) both represent this newer generation of zero-shot tabular models, offering predictions through a single forward pass rather than the conventional train-and-tune cycle.

A persistent limitation of both gradient boosting and transformer-based approaches, however, is their tendency to operate as opaque, uninterpretable systems. This research addresses that limitation by building a comparative machine learning framework for evaluating and explaining multi-tier hair fall risk, using structured clinical and lifestyle data. The study benchmarks three distinct modelling paradigms: CatBoost, a gradient-boosted decision tree architecture purpose-built for categorical data; TabPFN, an established prior-fitted transformer foundation model for tabular in-context learning; and TabFM, Google Research's more recently released zero-shot tabular foundation model. Post-hoc explainability is layered across all three models via Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP; Lundberg & Lee, 2017), giving each prediction a transparent, feature-level justification grounded in the underlying medical and lifestyle indicators.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

Determining hair loss risk remains a difficult clinical problem, shaped by the interplay of hormonal shifts, metabolic irregularities, chronic stress, nutritional gaps, and hair care habits. Assessment often relies on subjective self-reporting or delayed clinical evaluation, both of which are vulnerable to observer bias, inconsistent standards, and limited reach in under-resourced settings.

Machine learning can model these interacting risk factors systematically, but earlier studies that predict hair loss from survey and clinical tables have used only classical algorithms such as Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and their reported accuracies differ widely (Khatun et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2025; Siami & Azis, 2025). Newer tabular foundation models such as TabPFN and TabFM have been tested on general benchmarks (Hollmann et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2026) but not on hair loss data, and they have not been compared with a carefully tuned gradient-boosted model such as CatBoost. The predictions of both kinds of model are also difficult to explain, which limits their use in a clinical setting.

This study therefore builds and benchmarks an explainable machine learning framework for multi-tier hair fall risk classification. It compares CatBoost with the two tabular foundation models, TabPFN and TabFM, and uses SHAP-based feature attribution to keep every predicted risk tier traceable to specific, interpretable indicators.

## 1.3 Objectives

This dissertation has two objectives:

1. To implement and benchmark CatBoost, TabPFN, and TabFM for predicting multi-tier hair fall risk from structured clinical and lifestyle data.

2. To provide transparent, feature-level decision-support insights for every model through SHAP-based explainability, so that each predicted risk tier can be traced to specific clinical and lifestyle indicators.
